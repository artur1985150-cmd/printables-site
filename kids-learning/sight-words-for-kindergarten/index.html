Where kindergarten sight word lists come from

Sight words are the short, common words a reader recognises at once, without sounding them out. Two lists dominate American classrooms.

The Dolch list was put together by Edward William Dolch. It first appeared in a journal article in 1936 and then in his book Problems in Reading in 1948. It has 220 "service words" split by level, plus a separate list of 95 nouns. The Utah Education Network says these 220 words generally make up 50 to 75 percent of the reading material students meet.

Fry's Instant Words are ranked by how often they appear, most frequent first, and are usually taught in groups of 25.

List Words Level as named on the list
Dolch pre-primer 40 Pre-primer
Dolch primer 52 Primer
Dolch first grade 41 1st grade
Fry Instant Words, groups 1 to 4 100 First hundred

Kindergarten work usually draws on the first two Dolch levels or the first Fry hundred. Your child's school may use either one, a mix, or the word list that comes with its reading program.

Dolch pre-primer sight words (40 words)

These are the 40 pre-primer words as printed on the ReadWriteThink Dolch list, and they match the Wikipedia copy of the list word for word:

a, and, away, big, blue, can, come, down, find, for, funny, go, help, here, I, in, is, it, jump, little, look, make, me, my, not, one, play, red, run, said, see, the, three, to, two, up, we, where, yellow, you

Many of them are words a child can sound out once they know letter sounds (can, up, run, big). A few have a tricky spelling, such as said, the and one.

Dolch primer sight words for kindergarten (52 words)

The primer list is the one most often called "the Dolch kindergarten list". Again from the ReadWriteThink list, checked against the Wikipedia copy:

all, am, are, at, ate, be, black, brown, but, came, did, do, eat, four, get, good, have, he, into, like, must, new, no, now, on, our, out, please, pretty, ran, ride, saw, say, she, so, soon, that, there, they, this, too, under, want, was, well, went, what, white, who, will, with, yes

Together the two lists make 92 words. A natural order is pre-primer first, then primer once the first list is read without hesitation.

How many sight words should a kindergartener know?

No national rule sets a number. The Common Core standard for kindergarten (RF.K.3c) says children should "read common high-frequency words by sight", with examples such as the, of, to, you, she, my, is, are, do and does. It does not give a count.

Districts fill that gap themselves, and their numbers differ:

  • San Francisco Unified's kindergarten English page says students are expected to read and write all 76 of its high-frequency words independently by the end of the year, with two words taught per week.
  • A Brookfield, Connecticut elementary school's reading page says the expectation at the end of kindergarten is to master the first 50 high-frequency words, and 150 by the end of first grade.
  • Literacy researcher Timothy Shanahan writes that experts in a National Academy of Education report recommended that kindergartners master about 18 to 20 such words, including their names. He adds that sight word teaching "tends to be overdone".

So a child who knows 20 words and a child who knows 76 can both be on track, depending on the school. The useful question for your child's teacher is which list the class uses and which words are expected by when.

How to teach sight words in kindergarten

Reading Universe, WETA's teacher site, says experts now recommend that children memorise only the parts of a word that don't follow the phonics they have learned. It sorts high-frequency words into three kinds: decodable words like at and can, temporarily irregular words like for (until the child learns the "or" sound), and irregular words. Words with a tricky part are called heart words, because that part is learned "by heart". In from, a child can sound out f, r and m; only the o, which says /ŭ/, has to be remembered.

A simple routine at home. Steps 1 to 3 follow Reading Universe's routine for teaching the word was; steps 4 and 5 are our additions for home practice:

  1. Say the word and have your child repeat it.
  2. Ask what sounds they hear, and draw one line for each sound.
  3. Write the usual letter for each sound on its line; when a letter is unexpected, write it and draw a small heart under it.
  4. Have your child spell the word aloud, then read it.
  5. Use the word in a short spoken sentence.

On pace, Reading Universe suggests introducing up to three words a week in kindergarten, and notes that some teachers start with one irregular word a week. It also suggests grouping words that share a tricky part, such as could, would and should.

Keep each session short and review old words every day. A word counts as known when your child reads it straight away, not after a pause to sound it out.

Sight word games for kindergarten

Practice works better as a game than as a drill. Ideas that need little more than paper and a pen:

  • Sight word bingo: write five to nine known words on a grid, call them out, cover each one with a counter or a dot.
  • Word hunt: hide cards around the room; your child reads each one as they find it.
  • Memory: two cards for each word, face down; read the word when you turn it over.
  • Flashcard race: how many cards can your child read before a short song ends?
  • Stamp it: say a word and have your child find it on a page and stamp it with a dot marker.
  • Spot it in a book: pick one word before story time and count how often it appears.
  • Build it: make the word with magnetic letters, then mix the letters and build it again.

A dot marker page with sight words works well here; our dot marker activities guide has more page ideas. For a child who likes to sort and match, a sight word page fits into a busy book next to colour and shape pages.

Printable sight word practice at home

Printed sheets help because they are the same every time and easy to repeat. Ways to use them:

  1. Print the list your school uses and tick off words your child reads instantly.
  2. Make flashcards from the remaining words, three or so at a time.
  3. Laminate one page as a reusable wipe-clean tracing sheet. Our guide on how to make a busy book explains laminating and binding pages.
  4. Keep finished cards in a "known words" envelope and read through it once a week.

Sight words are one part of early reading. Letter sounds, rhyming and being read to are part of it too; our list of preschool activities has ideas for those, and the Kids Learning section has more.

Kindergarten sight words list: which one to print

If the school names a list, print that one. If it doesn't, a sensible order is Fry group 1, then the Dolch pre-primer words not yet covered, then the Dolch primer list. Both lists start with the same small words (the, a, and, to, is, you, it), so your child won't lose anything if the class later switches.

Questions people ask

What are the Dolch sight words for kindergarten?

Kindergarten usually covers the 40 Dolch pre-primer words (a, and, away, big, blue, can, come…) and the 52 primer words (all, am, are, at, ate, be, black, brown…). Both full lists are on this page.

How many sight words should a kindergartener know?

There is no national number. Common Core says only that kindergarteners should read common high-frequency words by sight. Districts set their own goals: San Francisco USD expects 76 words by the end of the year, and a Brookfield, Connecticut school expects the first 50. Ask your child's school which list it uses.

Should kindergarteners learn Dolch or Fry words?

Either works, and both start with the same small words. Some schools use Dolch pre-primer and primer for kindergarten; others use the first groups of Fry's Instant Words. Follow the list your child's class uses so practice at home matches school.

How many sight words should I teach a week?

Reading Universe suggests introducing up to three words a week in kindergarten, and says some teachers start with only one irregular word a week. Keep reviewing the old words every day.

What are heart words?

Heart words are high-frequency words with a tricky part that does not follow the phonics a child knows yet, so that part is learned by heart. In 'from', a child can sound out f, r and m; only the 'o' has to be remembered.
