In short

  • A busy book is a binder or cloth book of activity pages with pieces to match, post, lift, thread or fasten.
  • For one-year-olds, pick pages with big fixed parts: flaps to lift, a pocket to post into, a page to scribble on.
  • Threading, buttoning and story-order pages fit preschoolers; CDC lists stringing large beads at 3 and unbuttoning at 4.
  • In the US, toys meant for children under 3 may not contain small parts; keep loose pieces large or fixed.
  • Leave out magnets, button batteries and loose cords; the NHS warns about all three.

Good busy book ideas are pages a child can work with their hands: matching colours or shapes, posting pieces into a pocket, lifting flaps, tracing, threading a lace, fastening a button, putting a story in order. Pick pages for what the child can do now. For under-3s, keep every loose piece large or fixed in place.

Start with the table, then read the section for your child's age. The safety check at the end is short and worth doing every time you add a page.

What is a busy book?

A busy book, also called a quiet book, is a set of activity pages a child can play with on their own or next to you. Some are sewn from felt. Others are printed pages, laminated and kept in a ring binder, with pieces that stick on with hook-and-loop dots. Our guide to making a printable busy book walks through the binder version step by step.

The ideas on this page work for both. The difference is mostly how you make them.

Busy book ideas for 1 year olds

CDC's milestone checklists list what most children (75% or more) can do by a given age. By one year they include putting something in a container, like a block in a cup, and looking for a toy you hide under a blanket. They also include picking things up between thumb and pointer finger, which is exactly why small pieces need care at this age.

Pages that fit:

  • A lift-the-flap page: big felt or card flaps, each hiding a face, an animal or a familiar object.
  • A posting page: a pocket or a slot with two or three large cards to push in and pull out.
  • A texture page: patches of different fabrics stitched or glued down flat.
  • A peekaboo page with a mirror-card or a photo of a family member under a flap.

By 18 months the CDC list includes scribbling, so a laminated page and a chunky dry-erase crayon can join the book. Keep pen and marker caps out of reach: the American Academy of Pediatrics lists them among household choking hazards.

Toddler busy book ideas (around 2 years)

At two, the CDC list includes holding a container in one hand while taking the lid off with the other, trying switches, knobs and buttons on toys, and playing with more than one toy at once, such as putting toy food on a toy plate. That is a good fit for:

  • Colour matching with a handful of large pieces.
  • Shape matching onto outlines.
  • A "make a pizza" or "make a sandwich" page with toppings that stick on.
  • A simple dress-the-figure page.
  • A page with a real zip or a snap sewn on firmly.

By 30 months the checklist includes knowing at least one colour, following two-step instructions and turning book pages one at a time. Two-step games work well on matching pages: "find the red one and put it on the boat."

Busy book ideas for 3, 4 and 5 year olds

At three, the CDC list includes stringing large beads or macaroni and drawing a circle when you show how. At four it includes unbuttoning some buttons, naming a few colours, telling what comes next in a well-known story, and holding a crayon between fingers and thumb. Pages built around those:

  • Threading: big punched holes and a lace tied to the page at one end.
  • Buttons: large buttons sewn onto felt, with buttonholes to match.
  • Story order: three or four pictures from a story the child knows, put in order on a strip.
  • Tracing: lines, shapes and letters to trace with a dry-erase pen.
  • Counting: a ten-frame and small counters, or a "how many apples on the tree" page.
  • Dot pages: circles to stamp with a dot marker. Our list of dot marker activities has letter, number and seasonal ideas, and how to use dot markers covers the basics.

For school-age kids the same binder can hold mazes, puzzles and word games. When a busy book has to keep a group busy, our party games for kids list has quiet table games too.

Busy book theme ideas

A theme makes it easier to plan pages that hang together. These themes all turn up in Google and Bing search suggestions for busy books:

  • Farm: animals to match to their shadows, a barn with doors that open.
  • Ocean: fish to match by colour, a submarine with a window flap.
  • Dinosaurs: dinosaur shapes to match, eggs to count.
  • Vehicles: a road page with cars to park in numbered spaces.
  • Christmas: decorate a tree, match presents by colour.
  • Travel: a smaller book with fewer loose pieces, since a dropped piece in a car or on a plane is hard to get back.

Build the theme around what the child already likes. If they love trucks, a truck counting page beats a perfect page about something they ignore.

Printable or felt?

A printable busy book is quick to make: print, laminate, cut, add hook-and-loop dots and file the pages in a binder. A lost piece can be reprinted. A felt book is quieter and softer, takes longer to make, and lasts well if it is sewn carefully.

Many families mix the two: printed pages in a binder, with a felt page or a page of real fasteners slipped into a plastic sleeve. The step-by-step guide to how to make a busy book covers materials, pouch thickness and how to store the pieces.

Safety checks before you hand it over

Busy books are home-made toys, so nobody has tested yours. Run this check:

  1. Under 3? In the US, toys and children's products meant for children under 3 may not contain small parts that pose a choking risk (CPSC). Keep loose pieces large, or fix them to the page.
  2. Follow age labels on bought parts. Pompoms, beads and stick-on eyes often come in packs labelled for older children. The AAP says age guidelines on toys are based on choking hazards as well as a child's abilities.
  3. No button batteries. The NHS warns that they can cause severe internal burns if swallowed. Skip light-up or sound pieces unless the battery is behind a screwed-shut cover.
  4. No small magnets. The NHS warns that swallowed magnets can stick together in a child's intestines.
  5. Short, fixed laces. The NHS advises against leaving any rope or cord lying around a baby or toddler. Tie laces to the page and keep them short.
  6. Check wear. Look at stitches, hook-and-loop dots and laminated edges each time. A piece that comes loose becomes a loose small part.
  7. Watch older siblings' pages. The AAP notes that many choking incidents happen when an older child gives a younger one a small object.

Questions people ask

What do you put in a busy book?

Activity pages a child can work with their hands: matching colours or shapes, posting pieces into a pocket, lifting flaps, tracing with a dry-erase pen, threading a lace, fastening a zip or button, putting story pictures in order. Choose pages for what the child can do now and add harder ones later.

What busy book pages suit a 1 year old?

Pages without small loose parts: big flaps to lift, a pocket with one or two large pieces to post, a textured page to touch, a page to scribble on. CDC's 1-year checklist includes putting something in a container and looking for a toy you hide, which is what a posting page and a flap page ask for.

At what age can a child use a busy book with small pieces?

The US small-parts rule covers products meant for children under 3, and the AAP says to follow the age recommendations on toy packages. Keep loose pieces large for under-3s, and keep pages with small pieces away from younger brothers and sisters.

Is a printable busy book as good as a felt one?

They suit different jobs. A laminated printable binder is quick to make and a lost piece can be reprinted. A felt book is quieter and softer but takes cutting and sewing. Many families start with printables and add a felt page or two.

How many pages should a busy book have?

There is no set number. A binder lets you swap pages in and out, so a handful of pages the child likes, changed now and then, works better than a thick book handed over all at once.
