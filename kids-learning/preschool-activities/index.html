In short

  • Pick activities by skill: fine motor, early literacy, numbers, movement and art, and keep each one short.
  • CDC milestones show what most children do by 3, 4 and 5, such as stringing large beads at 3, drawing a person at 4 and counting to 10 at 5.
  • The NHS says children aged 3 to 4 should be active at least 180 minutes a day, including 60 minutes of energetic play.
  • Crayons, chalk, paints and clay are exempt from the US small-parts ban for under-3s; the AAP lists balloons, marbles, small balls and pen caps as choking hazards.
  • The AAP's January 2026 media policy replaces its earlier guidance and stresses a family media plan and screen-free times such as meals.

Good preschool activities are short, hands-on and aimed at one skill at a time: playdough and threading for little fingers, rhymes and name letters for early reading, counting and sorting for numbers, running and ball games for movement, and drawing or pretend play for art. Pick ones your child can nearly do already, keep them to a few minutes, and keep small parts away from toddlers.

The ideas below are grouped by skill, with the CDC milestones that show what most children manage at 3, 4 and 5.

Fine motor activities for preschoolers

Small hand muscles get stronger with lots of short, repeated use. At 3, the CDC lists stringing large beads or macaroni; by 4, holding a crayon between fingers and thumb instead of in a fist, and unbuttoning some buttons.

  • Threading large beads, pasta tubes or punched card shapes onto a shoelace.
  • Rolling, squashing and cutting playdough with a blunt knife.
  • Moving pom-poms or cotton balls from bowl to bowl with kitchen tongs.
  • Peeling and placing stickers on dots or lines.
  • Tearing paper strips and gluing them into a collage.
  • Practising buttons and zips on an old coat.
  • Dot marker pages, where a child presses a marker onto each circle; see our dot marker activities for page ideas by season.

Pages with flaps, hook-and-loop pieces and zips do the same job in book form. Our busy book ideas list pages by age, and how to make a busy book covers laminating and binding them.

Early literacy activities at home

Reading starts with talk, sounds and stories long before letters. The CDC lists saying some words from a song, story or nursery rhyme by 4, and naming some letters, writing some letters of their name and using simple rhymes (bat-cat, ball-tall) by 5.

  1. Read aloud every day, and ask what is happening in the pictures.
  2. Play "I spy" with first sounds: "I spy something that starts with /m/".
  3. Make up silly rhymes in the car or the bath.
  4. Write your child's name on cards and look for its letters on signs and packets.
  5. Ask your child to retell a favourite story; by 5, the CDC lists telling a story with at least two events.

Children heading into kindergarten will meet sight words next; our guide to sight words for kindergarten has the Dolch and Fry lists.

Math and number activities for preschoolers

By 5, the CDC lists counting to 10 and naming some numbers between 1 and 5 when you point to them. Colours come earlier: naming a few colours of items is on the 4-year list.

  • Count stairs, spoons at the table or cars that pass.
  • Sort a pile of socks, buttons or blocks by colour, then by size.
  • Make a pattern with blocks (red, blue, red, blue) and ask what comes next.
  • Play a simple dice board game, counting each move aloud.
  • Fill and pour with cups in the bath: which holds more?

Gross motor activities and how much to move

The NHS says children aged 3 to 4 should spend at least 180 minutes (3 hours) a day doing a variety of physical activities spread through the day, and that this should include at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity. The CDC says children aged 3 to 5 should be physically active throughout the day and suggests encouraging activity during play, such as jumping or riding a tricycle.

Ideas for indoors and out:

  • Throwing and catching a large soft ball (the CDC lists catching a large ball most of the time by 4).
  • Hopping games, such as hopscotch chalked on a path (hopping on one foot is on the CDC's 5-year list).
  • Obstacle courses with cushions to climb over and a table to crawl under.
  • Dancing to music and freezing when it stops.
  • Animal walks: waddle like a penguin, jump like a frog.

Art and pretend play activities

Let your child choose colours and tools, and skip the finished model to copy. The CDC lists drawing a circle when shown how by 3, and drawing a person with three or more body parts by 4.

  • Painting with brushes, sponges, fingers or cut vegetables.
  • Drawing with chunky crayons or chalk outdoors.
  • Collage with fabric scraps, paper shapes and leaves.
  • Dress-up and role play; the CDC lists pretending to be something else, such as a teacher or a superhero, by 4.
  • Puppet shows with paper bags or socks.

How long should preschool activities last?

Shorter than you'd think. By 5, the CDC lists paying attention for 5 to 10 minutes during activities such as story time or arts and crafts, and notes that screen time does not count. Younger children often manage less.

So plan a few short activities rather than one long one, have the next one ready, and stop while your child still wants more. Mix quiet table activities with active ones through the day.

Safety: small parts, toddler siblings and screens

Some craft supplies are exempt from the US rule on small parts. The CPSC's small-parts ban applies to products intended for children under 3, but it exempts writing materials such as crayons, chalk, pencils and pens, fingerpaints, watercolours and other paint sets, and modeling clay. In practice, that means an art box can hold pieces small enough for a toddler sibling to choke on.

The AAP's choking prevention page lists objects to keep away from infants and children, including:

  • Balloons, which it says are a choking risk up to 8 years of age.
  • Coins, marbles and small balls.
  • Toys with small parts, and toys that can be squeezed to fit entirely into a mouth.
  • Pen or marker caps.
  • Button batteries.

The same page lists foods to keep away until age 4 or older, among them marshmallows, popcorn, whole grapes and hard or sticky candy. Skip them in sensory bins and counting games if a younger child is around. If your preschooler still puts things in their mouth, use larger pieces and supervise closely.

On screens, the AAP published a new policy on children and digital media in January 2026 that replaces its earlier media guidance. Its parent summary recommends a family media plan and spaces and times free of digital media, such as the car, dinnertime or weekends, and gives no hour limit. The earlier advice, still on HealthyChildren.org's food and media page (updated 2024), was no more than one hour a day from 18 to 24 months on. Questions people ask

What are good learning activities for preschoolers?

Short, hands-on ones: playdough and tongs for fine motor skills, rhymes and name letters for early literacy, counting and sorting for numbers, ball games and hopping for movement, and drawing and pretend play. Match them to what your child can already nearly do.

How long should preschool activities last?

Keep them short. The CDC lists paying attention for 5 to 10 minutes during activities such as story time or arts and crafts as a milestone by age 5, so plan several short activities and stop while it is still fun.

How much physical activity does a preschooler need?

The NHS says children aged 3 to 4 should spend at least 180 minutes a day being active, spread through the day, including at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity. The CDC says children aged 3 to 5 should be physically active throughout the day.

Are preschool craft supplies safe for toddlers?

Not always. The US small-parts ban covers products meant for children under 3, but crayons, chalk, paints and modeling clay are exempt. Supervise closely, and keep beads, pom-poms, marbles, pen caps and balloons away from younger siblings.

How much screen time is OK for a preschooler?

The AAP's January 2026 policy, which replaces its earlier media guidance, recommends a family media plan and screen-free spaces and times such as meals, the car or weekends; its parent summary gives no hour limit. The earlier AAP advice, still on HealthyChildren.org, was no more than one hour a day from 18 to 24 months on.
