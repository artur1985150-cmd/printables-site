In short

  • A printable busy book is printed pages, laminated, cut and filed in a ring binder, with pieces held on by hook-and-loop dots.
  • Base pages bend in a binder, so 3 mil or 5 mil pouches suit them; loose pieces hold up better in 5 mil.
  • Check your laminator's maximum pouch thickness first; many basic machines stop at 5 or 7 mil.
  • For children under 3, keep every loose piece large; the US small-parts rule covers products meant for that age.
  • Store each page's pieces in an envelope filed next to the page, and check pieces for wear.

To make a busy book for toddlers, print your activity pages, laminate them, cut out the loose pieces, add hook-and-loop dots to the pieces and the pages, and file everything in a ring binder. Keep every loose piece large for a child under 3, and store each page's pieces in an envelope next to it.

The steps below are for a printable binder. A felt version is at the end.

What you need

  • Printable busy book pages. If you have not chosen pages yet, start with our busy book ideas, sorted by age.
  • A printer and ordinary paper or light card.
  • A pouch laminator and pouches (thickness below).
  • Sharp scissors, or a paper trimmer for straight edges.
  • Hook-and-loop dots, the self-adhesive kind.
  • A hole punch that matches your binder's rings.
  • A sturdy ring binder.
  • Small envelopes or zip pouches for loose pieces.

Teaching Littles, whose busy binder tutorial uses the same kit, notes that plain copy paper is fine when the pages will be laminated.

Which laminating pouch thickness to use

Pouch thickness is given in mil, thousandths of an inch. Because film covers both sides, the finished sheet is about twice the rated number. MyBinding's guide describes the common thicknesses like this:

Pouch How the finished sheet feels In a busy book
3 mil Thin, bends and curves with handling Base pages that live in the binder
5 mil Semi-rigid, holds its shape Base pages and loose pieces
7 mil Noticeably stiffer Pieces that get bent a lot, if your machine takes it
10 mil Fully rigid, like a credit card Rarely needed; needs a machine rated for it

The same guide says most basic machines top out at 5 or 7 mil, so check your laminator's rating before you buy pouches. For 10 mil it says you need a variable-temperature machine set to its highest setting.

Step by step

  1. Print the pages. Print base pages and piece sheets separately so you can laminate them differently.
  2. Laminate the base pages. If a base page has activities on both sides, print it double-sided or put two sheets back to back in one pouch.
  3. Punch the base pages and file them in the binder.
  4. Laminate the piece sheets, one-sided.
  5. Cut out the pieces. Trim close to the picture, then run a fingertip round the edge. If a corner feels sharp, round it off with the scissors.
  6. Stick the hook-and-loop dots on. Put one side on each spot of the base page where a piece belongs, and the matching side on the back of each piece. Use the same side (hook or loop) on all base pages so any piece fits any page.
  7. Press each piece onto its place and leave it there for storage.

Teaching Littles suggests handing over one page at a time, out of the binder, so the rings do not get in the way during play.

Make pieces big enough

In the US, toys and children's products intended for children under 3 may not contain small parts that pose a choking, aspiration or ingestion risk. The CPSC defines a small part by whether it fits entirely into a standard test cylinder. You do not need the cylinder to apply the idea at home: if you are making a book for a child under 3, keep every loose piece clearly larger than anything they could fit in their mouth, or fix the piece to the page.

CDC's one-year milestone list includes picking things up between thumb and pointer finger, like small bits of food. Toddlers handle small things early, so size is your first safety step, and supervision the second.

Storing pieces so they don't get lost

  • Put each page's pieces in a small envelope, punch it, and file it right after that page.
  • Or clip the envelope to the page.
  • Leave pieces stuck on the page between uses; the child pulls them off to start.
  • Count the pieces when you put a page away. A missing piece is one you want to find before a baby does.

A felt busy book instead

Print the same templates, pin them to felt, cut round them and sew or glue the shapes in place. Felt pages can go into a binder too, through punched holes reinforced with a strip of card, or be stitched together into a cloth book. Hook-and-loop dots work on felt the same way. The page ideas are the same; a felt book takes longer and is quieter in a car or a waiting room.

Safety check before the first play

  1. Pieces large enough for the child's age (above).
  2. No button batteries. The NHS warns that they can cause severe internal burns if swallowed.
  3. No small magnets. The NHS warns that swallowed magnets can stick together in a child's intestines.
  4. Laces and ribbons short and tied to the page. The NHS advises against leaving cords lying around babies and toddlers.
  5. No pen or marker caps left loose: the AAP lists them among household choking hazards.
  6. Check the book each time: peeling dots, split laminate and torn felt all turn fixed parts into loose ones.

Pages to add as the child grows

Start with a few pages and add harder ones over time: threading, buttons, story order, tracing, counting. Laminated dot pages slot straight in; see our dot marker activities for page ideas and how to use dot markers for the basics. Questions people ask

What do I need to make a busy book?

Printable pages, a printer, a laminator and pouches, scissors, hook-and-loop dots, a hole punch and a ring binder. Envelopes or zip pouches for the pieces help keep each page's set together.

What thickness of laminating pouch is best for a busy book?

A binding supplier's guide describes 3 mil as thin and flexible and 5 mil as semi-rigid, firm enough to hold its shape. Base pages that sit in a binder work in either; loose pieces that little hands bend and pull are sturdier in 5 mil. Check your laminator's limit first.

Do I need a laminator to make a busy book?

It helps, because laminated pages wipe clean and pieces last longer. Without one, print on card and slip base pages into clear binder sleeves, but loose paper pieces will wear out faster.

How do I stop busy book pieces getting lost?

Keep one page's pieces in a small envelope or zip pouch punched and filed next to that page, or clipped to it. Give the child one page at a time rather than the whole binder.

Can I make a felt busy book instead?

Yes. Print the same templates, trace the shapes onto felt, cut them out and sew or glue the base pieces to felt pages. It takes longer but makes a soft, quiet book.
