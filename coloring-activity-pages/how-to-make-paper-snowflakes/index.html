In short

  • Real snow crystals have six arms, so fold for six: square, triangle, half again, then thirds.
  • The point of the folded wedge is the centre of the snowflake; the trimmed top becomes its outer edge.
  • Cut into both long edges, but leave part of each folded edge uncut, or the snowflake falls apart.
  • Printer paper folds and cuts well; card stock is too thick once it is folded into many layers.
  • Flatten finished snowflakes inside a large book for a day or two, or smooth the creases with an iron on low heat.

To make a paper snowflake, cut a square of thin paper, fold it into a triangle, fold that in half, then fold it into thirds so it becomes a narrow wedge. Trim the top, cut small shapes into both long edges, and unfold. The thirds fold gives six arms, like a real snowflake. Below are the steps, a map of the folded wedge, and what to do when a snowflake falls apart.

What you need

  • Paper. Printer paper, construction paper, parchment paper or newsprint all work, according to Good Housekeeping. A Piece of Rainbow warns against card stock: thick paper is hard to cut once it is folded.
  • Sharp scissors. Good Housekeeping notes that some children's scissors are too dull, depending on the paper.
  • A pencil, for drawing the cuts before you make them.
  • A ruler, if you want neat edges on the square.

How to fold a paper snowflake with six points

SnowCrystals.com says you "really ought" to make paper snowflakes six-sided, because real snowflakes are never eight-sided. Here is the most common fold, as given by A Piece of Rainbow and Good Housekeeping:

  1. Make a square. Fold one corner of a sheet of Letter paper down to the opposite edge, forming a triangle, and cut off the strip below it.
  2. Keep it folded as a triangle, with the long folded edge at the bottom.
  3. Fold the triangle in half, point to point, to make a smaller triangle.
  4. Fold that smaller triangle into thirds from its point. Fold one third over, then the other over the top of it, and match the edges as closely as you can.
  5. Flatten the top by cutting off the uneven points, as Good Housekeeping does.

Step 4 is the hard one. A Piece of Rainbow divides the 90-degree corner into three equal parts of 30 degrees each, and says estimating by eye is fine. Before you crease, push the paper around until both outer edges line up, then flatten it.

The SnowCrystals.com diagram starts differently: fold an 8.5 by 11 sheet in half, fold it in half again, then make the one-third fold, fold the remainder over the first third and cut off the excess. Both ways give the same six-armed result.

Which part of the wedge becomes what

Once the paper is folded, it helps to know what you are looking at before you cut:

Part of the folded wedge What it becomes
The narrow point at the bottom The centre of the snowflake
The top you trimmed The outer edge of the snowflake

Dummies puts it plainly: the bottom point is the middle of your snowflake. So a cut near the point goes through the centre, and cuts along the long edges open up between the centre and the outer edge. Sketch the cuts in pencil first to see where they will land.

How to cut paper snowflake patterns

Draw the cuts in pencil first. Good Housekeeping suggests marking the parts you plan to remove with an X, so you do not cut away the part you meant to keep.

Shapes to try:

  • Small triangles and half circles along both long edges.
  • A slanted cut across the top, for pointed arms, or a V-shape, for forked arms.
  • Thin slits cut in from the edge, close together, for a lacy look.
  • A tiny snip off the bottom point, for a hole in the centre.

The one rule: never cut all the way across from one long edge to the other. Leave a strip of the folded edge uncut at several points, or the arms come away from the centre when you open it.

Unfold slowly. Thin paper tears at the narrow joins if you pull it open in one go.

Make it look like a real snowflake

SnowCrystals.com challenges paper snowflake makers to copy real features of snow crystals. It lists three: two concentric hexagons in the centre, central ridges on the arms, and side branches coming off the arms at 60-degree angles.

On the folded wedge, that means:

  • Short cuts across the wedge near the point, for the hexagon rings. Do not cut them all the way across.
  • A thin slit along a folded side, for the central ridge.
  • Short angled cuts off the edge, for side branches.

The site warns that cutting anatomically correct snowflakes is harder than it sounds. It suggests using its four real snowflake photos as models, getting the outline right first and adding the inner features after.

On its fun facts page, SnowCrystals.com says Frances Chickering, a minister's wife from Maine, published snow-crystal images in her 1864 book Cloud Crystals: A Snow-Flake Album. She cut paper replicas of crystals as they fell on her windowsill, and the site calls it the craft she was pioneering.

Easy paper snowflakes for kids

For young children, an adult can do the folding and the first trim, when the paper is thickest. Children then cut simple shapes along the edges.

What makes it easier:

  • Start with a square made from a full 8.5 by 11 sheet, as A Piece of Rainbow does. Bigger wedges leave more room for small hands.
  • Draw two or three cuts on the wedge and let the child follow the lines.
  • Skip the thirds and fold into quarters instead. A Piece of Rainbow gives this fold for eight-pointed snowflakes, even if real snow never looks like that.

Why your paper snowflake went wrong

What happened Why Fix next time
It fell into pieces A cut went all the way across the wedge Leave uncut sections on both long edges
It has eight arms, not six The triangle was folded into quarters, not thirds Fold into thirds from the point
The arms are uneven The thirds were not equal when creased Line up the outer edges before flattening
The cuts are ragged Paper too thick, or scissors too dull Use printer paper and sharper scissors
A big hole in the middle Too much cut off the bottom point Snip only the very tip

Flatten, stiffen and hang paper snowflakes

Folded snowflakes curl. A Piece of Rainbow presses them between the pages of a large book or magazine for a day or two. Good Housekeeping suggests a low-heat iron setting to smooth out creases.

For stiffer snowflakes, A Piece of Rainbow brushes on a mix of 1 part water, 2 parts cornstarch and 2 parts white glue, lets one side dry on a sheet of plastic, then does the other side.

To display them, tape them to windows, walls or doors, as A Piece of Rainbow does, or chain them into a garland, as Good Housekeeping suggests. They also work on gift wrap and cards.

Using a printable snowflake template

A template is a drawing of the cuts that you print, cut out and trace onto your folded wedge, or print straight onto paper and fold along the guide. It saves guessing at the angles.

If the template is a PDF, print it at actual size, so the wedge matches the fold it was drawn for; our guide on how to print wall art shows where that setting is. Print on thin paper, since you will fold it into many layers.

Questions people ask

How do you make a paper snowflake step by step?

Cut a square from a sheet of paper. Fold it corner to corner into a triangle, then fold that triangle in half. Fold the new triangle into thirds from the point, so it looks like a narrow wedge. Cut off the uneven top, cut small shapes into both long edges, and unfold slowly.

Why do paper snowflakes have six points?

Because real snow crystals do. SnowCrystals.com explains that the water molecules in a snow crystal are lined up in a hexagonal array, and a stellar crystal starts as a small hexagonal plate with branches sprouting from its six corners. The site also says real snowflakes are never eight-sided, which is why the thirds fold is worth learning.

What paper is best for paper snowflakes?

Printer paper, construction paper, parchment or newsprint, according to Good Housekeeping. A Piece of Rainbow warns against card stock, because thick paper is hard to cut once it is folded. Sharp scissors matter as much as the paper; Good Housekeeping notes some children's scissors are too dull.

How do you flatten paper snowflakes?

Press them between the pages of a large book or magazine for a day or two, or smooth the creases with an iron on a low heat setting.

How do you make an 8-pointed paper snowflake?

Follow the same steps, but fold the small triangle into quarters instead of thirds before cutting. A Piece of Rainbow gives this fold for eight-pointed snowflakes, though real snow crystals are never eight-sided.
