What are dot markers?

Dot markers are squeezable bottles of liquid paint with a round sponge tip. You'll also see them called daubers or bingo daubers. Pressing the tip onto paper leaves a round dot about the width of the tip, so a child can make a neat mark without any drawing skill.

The name also covers a different tool: the fine dot pens adults use in planners and bullet journals. This guide is about the kids' paint bottles.

How to use dot markers, step by step

  1. Cover the table. A tray, an old placemat or newspaper under the paper, and an old shirt or apron on the child.
  2. Take one cap off at a time. Fewer open bottles means fewer caps on the floor.
  3. Hold the bottle upright, tip down, like a stamp.
  4. Press straight down and lift. Dragging it smears the dot.
  5. Move to the next spot. On a dot marker page, that's the next circle. On plain paper, anywhere.
  6. Cap it again before switching colours, and check the cap is on firmly.

If a new marker won't leave a dot, test it on scrap paper and then check the maker's instructions for that set.

What to do with them

Most children start with plain paper and free stamping, then move to printed pages. Our list of dot marker activities has picture, letter, number and seasonal pages, a theme for every month, and ideas that need no printer.

A few simple ones to start:

  • Fill every circle on a big shape: a sun, a ball, an apple.
  • Make a row of dots in two colours and take turns adding the next one.
  • Stamp dots all over a page, then turn them into faces with a pen.

No dot markers? Stand-ins that work

Dot marker pages don't need dot markers. Round stickers fill the circles just as well and leave no paint on the table. Speech Blubs suggests peeling away the white background around the stickers on the sheet first, so small fingers can lift each dot more easily. A cotton bud dipped in paint stamps a smaller dot, and crayons or chunky pencils can colour the circles in. Any of these lets a child try the same pages before you buy a set, or while a younger brother or sister is at the table.

Paper and surfaces

Dot markers lay down wet paint, so thin printer paper can buckle or soak through to the table. Heavier paper or card stock copes better. If you print dot pages, print at actual size so the circles stay as big as the designer made them, and check that they are at least as wide as your marker's dot.

Laminated pages are better kept for dot stickers or dry-erase pens; they also slot into a busy book binder.

Reading the label: age, AP seal, ASTM D-4236

Read the box before the first use. These are the lines that matter.

The age label. Do-A-Dot Art sets listed by Target and by Becker's School Supplies say "3 years and up" and warn: "Choking hazard, small parts. Not for children under 3." The CPSC explains that products meant for children under 3 may not contain small parts, and products for older children that do must carry this kind of warning.

The AP seal. The Art & Creative Materials Institute (ACMI) gives its Approved Product seal to art materials that a toxicologist has found to contain nothing in sufficient quantities to be toxic or injurious to humans, including children. ACMI says to look for the AP seal for children in sixth grade or younger, and never to give them a product with its CL (Cautionary Labeling) seal.

"Conforms to ASTM D-4236". The CPSC says every art material sold in the US must carry this statement, whether or not it poses a hazard. It means the product has been reviewed for chronic hazards and labelled accordingly. On its own it does not tell you the product is non-toxic.

Safety with small children

  • Stay with toddlers while they use any art materials.
  • Keep caps out of reach. The American Academy of Pediatrics lists pen and marker caps among household choking hazards.
  • Follow the age label. The AAP says age guidelines on toys reflect choking hazards as well as a child's abilities.
  • If paint gets in a child's mouth or eyes, follow the first-aid advice on the package, and call your doctor or poison centre if you're worried.

Clean-up and storage

"Washable" is the maker's claim about skin and clothes; how well it works varies, so follow that set's washing instructions and treat marks quickly. For storage, Speech Blubs suggests capping tightly so the sponge tips don't dry out and storing the markers on their side to avoid leaks. Some makers say their tips won't dry out even uncapped, so go by your own set's instructions.

Dot pages also make a quiet page for a busy book, or a calm table between party games for kids.

Questions people ask

What are dot markers?

Dot markers, also called daubers or bingo daubers, are squeezable bottles of liquid paint with a round sponge tip. Pressed onto paper, the tip stamps a round dot. Kids use them on dot marker pages, where a picture is made of circles to fill.

How do you use dot markers for the first time?

Take the cap off, hold the bottle upright like a stamp, press the tip straight down onto the paper and lift. One press makes one dot. Put the cap back when you're done.

What age are dot markers for?

Read the box. Do-A-Dot Art sets listed by Target and Becker's are marked 3 years and up, with a warning that they contain small parts and are not for children under 3. If a younger child uses art materials, stay with them.

Are dot markers non-toxic?

Many sets say so. The clearer sign is the ACMI AP seal, which ACMI gives to art materials a toxicologist has found not to contain anything in amounts toxic to people, including children. The line 'Conforms to ASTM D-4236' is required on every art material in the US, so it is not a non-toxic claim by itself.

Should dot markers be stored upside down?

Follow your set's instructions. Some advice says to store them on their side and cap them tightly so the tips don't dry out; some makers say their tips won't dry out uncapped. Sets differ.
