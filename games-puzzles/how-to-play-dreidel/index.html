In short

  • Share out the tokens, everyone puts one in the pot, then take turns spinning.
  • Nun: nothing. Gimel: take the whole pot. Hei: take half. Shin (or pey in Israel): put one in.
  • When the pot is empty or down to one piece, everyone adds one; a player who can't pay is out.
  • Sources disagree on odd halves and re-antes, so agree on house rules before the first spin.
  • For a shorter game, start with fewer pieces each or set a timer and let the biggest pile win.

To play dreidel, share the tokens out evenly, have everyone put one in the middle, and take turns spinning the four-sided top. The letter that lands face up tells you what to do: nun, nothing; gimel, take the whole pot; hei, take half; shin, put one piece in. When a player runs out of pieces they are out, and the last player left holding pieces wins.

What you need to play dreidel

One dreidel, a flat table and something to bet with. Chabad.org lists nuts, pennies, nickels and chocolate coins; My Jewish Learning adds raisins, chocolate chips and matchsticks. Chocolate gelt works well, since you can eat your winnings at the end.

My Jewish Learning says any number can play.

How many pieces each? My Jewish Learning suggests 10 to 15 per player. For a game that fits before dinner, see the short version below.

Dreidel rules step by step

  1. Share out the tokens so everyone has the same number.
  2. Decide who goes first. Chabad.org has each player spin once: the highest letter starts, in the order nun, gimel, hei, shin. A tie spins again.
  3. Everyone puts one piece into the pot.
  4. The first player spins. Do what the letter says, then pass the dreidel to the left, so play goes clockwise.
  5. When the pot is empty, or has only one piece in it, everyone puts in one more.
  6. If you land on shin, or the pot needs refilling, and you have nothing left, you are out.
  7. The game ends when one player has everything.

The dreidel has to spin on its own and fall over. A top that skids off the table or lands leaning on a cup gets spun again; agree on that before you start.

What the dreidel letters mean

Letter Hebrew What you do Word it comes from
Nun נ Nothing. Pass the dreidel. nisht, "nothing"
Gimel ג Take the whole pot. Then everyone puts one back in. gantz, "whole"
Hei ה Take half the pot. halb, "half"
Shin ש Put one piece in. shtel arayn, "put in" (Chabad: shenk, "give")
Pey (Israel) פ Same as shin.

Read as Hebrew, the letters stand for "nes gadol hayah sham": a great miracle happened there. Dreidels made in Israel swap the shin for a pey, for "po", meaning "here". Exploring Judaism and Chabad.org both explain the swap. A pey dreidel plays exactly like a shin dreidel.

Before the first spin, settle three things out loud: what hei does with an odd pot, whether loans are allowed, and when the game stops. That is the whole of the house rules most families need.

How to make a dreidel game shorter

Dreidel games can run on and on. Wikipedia cites a study by Thomas Robinson and Sujith Vijay showing that the expected number of spins is bounded by the square of the pieces each player starts with. So doubling the pieces can make the game up to about four times longer.

Ways to keep it short, all of them our own house rules:

  • Start with 5 to 8 pieces each instead of 10 to 15.
  • Raise the stakes. Chabad.org suggests paying two, three or even four pieces on shin and on the refill after a gimel.
  • Set a timer for 15 minutes. When it rings, the biggest pile wins.
  • Play to a target: the first player to double their starting pile wins.
  • Let players who are out keep spinning for the pot without paying, so nobody sits out for long.

There is a second, smaller problem. Wikipedia also cites Robert Feinerman, who showed the game is unfair: the first spinner has a better expected result than the second, the second better than the third. If you play several rounds, let a different person start each time.

Dreidel for young kids

Small children love the spinning more than the betting. Our simpler version for preschoolers and early readers:

  • Give everyone the same small pile, five pieces is enough.
  • Skip "half". Hei means "take two" instead, which avoids sums.
  • Nobody goes out. If you are empty, you just spin and hope for gimel.
  • Stop after one round of the table per player, or when the snack runs out.

With a baby or toddler at the table, use bigger tokens, such as wooden blocks or crackers, or keep the pot out of reach between turns.

Where dreidel comes from

The dreidel is older than its Hanukkah meaning. In his article on My Jewish Learning, Rabbi David Golinkin traces it to a European spinning-top game: by 1720 it was called a "teetotum", and by 1801 an English version had the letters T for take all, H for half, P for put down and N for nothing. The German version used N for nichts, G for ganz, H for halb and S for stell ein, and the Hebrew letters on the dreidel follow those German ones.

Chabad.org tells the traditional story: when the Greek-Syrian rulers banned Torah study, children learned in hiding and pulled out small tops to look as if they were only playing. Golinkin writes that explanations of this kind were added later. In modern Hebrew the dreidel is called a sevivon.

Questions people ask

What are the rules of dreidel?

Divide the tokens evenly, and each player puts one into the pot. Players spin in turn, clockwise. Nun means nothing happens, gimel means you take the whole pot, hei means you take half, and shin means you put one piece in. When the pot runs dry, everyone adds one. A player who can't pay is out, and the last player with pieces wins.

What do the four letters on a dreidel mean?

Nun, gimel, hei and shin stand for 'nes gadol hayah sham', 'a great miracle happened there'. Dreidels made in Israel use pey instead of shin, for 'a great miracle happened here'. In the game, the letters are read as Yiddish or German words: nothing, whole, half and put in.

How many pieces does each player start with?

My Jewish Learning suggests 10 to 15 each. Fewer pieces make a much shorter game, so 5 to 8 each works well with young children or when you only have a few minutes.

What happens if you land on hei and the pot has an odd number?

It depends on whose rules you follow. Chabad.org leaves the odd piece in the pot, while My Jewish Learning and Wikipedia have the player round up and take the larger half. Pick one before you start.

When is Hanukkah 2026?

Hebcal lists Hanukkah 2026 from sundown on Friday, December 4, to nightfall on Saturday, December 12, 2026. The first candle is lit on the evening of December 4.
