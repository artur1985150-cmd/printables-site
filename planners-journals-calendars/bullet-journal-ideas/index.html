The best bullet journal ideas fit the method's own structure. Start with the four core pages from bulletjournal.com: an Index, a Future Log, a Monthly Log and a Daily Log. What is the Bullet Journal method?

The Bullet Journal method was created by Ryder Carroll. According to its official site, it began as his personal way to stay on top of tasks and record ideas, and he developed it as a tool to help with his ADHD before sharing it with others.

It runs on rapid logging: short, bulleted entries that show what kind of thing each line is. The official site describes the basics like this:

Symbol Meaning
• (dot) A task
X A finished task
> or → A task moved elsewhere (migrated)
Struck through A task you no longer need
O An event
– (dash) A note

The method also adds signifiers to the left of a bullet. The official site's examples are an asterisk for a priority task and an exclamation mark for an idea. You can change the bullets to suit you; the official site says so.

Bullet journal ideas for beginners: the core pages

Everything in a bullet journal is a collection, and four are core. Set them up in this order:

  1. The Index. Leave the first couple of pages blank and title them "Index". As you use the book, write each topic and its page numbers here, for example "New Zealand Trip: 5-10".
  2. The Future Log. Divide a spread into blocks for the months ahead. The official example uses two evenly spaced horizontal lines across facing pages, which gives six boxes, one per month. Dates and plans further out than this month go here.
  3. The Monthly Log. A double-page spread per month. On the left, the month name at the top and the dates (1 to 30 or 31) down the side. On the right, a task list for the month.
  4. The Daily Log. Write today's date as a heading and rapid-log tasks, events and notes under it.

The official site also describes an optional Weekly Log: a double-page spread with the week's dates and appointments on the left and tasks and notes on the right.

Number your pages before you start. The Index only works if every page has a number.

Bullet journal page ideas: collections worth starting

Once the core pages are in place, each new idea becomes a collection. The method's own instructions are short: flip to the next blank spread, give it a topic, move any related tasks and notes into it, and add the topic and page number to your Index.

Collections people keep coming back to:

  • Habit tracker: a grid of days across the top and habits down the side
  • Reading list and books finished
  • Films and series to watch
  • Meal plan and shopping list
  • Budget and bills due
  • Birthdays by month
  • Trip planning and a packing list
  • Gift ideas through the year
  • A project page for a move, a course or a renovation
  • Notes for a class or a long-running work project
  • A brain dump page for anything that doesn't fit yet

Start with one. A collection you fill every week is worth more than ten decorated pages you never open.

The official site also points out that people highlight their Monthly Logs in the Index to make a quick timeline of the year. It is a small trick that makes a full notebook easy to scan.

How to keep a big bullet journal easy to use

The Index is the method's answer to "where did I write that?". Two more habits from the official site help once the notebook fills up:

  • Continue, don't cram. If a collection runs out of room, continue it on the next free spread with the same title and add the new page numbers to the Index.
  • Thread your collections. Next to a collection's page number, write the page number of its previous or next part. You can then jump straight from one part to the next without going back to the Index.
  • Use the monthly migration. At the start of each month, move unfinished tasks forward. The official site calls this a good time to create and update collections, too.
  • Give big projects their own index. The method allows a dedicated Index for a long project or for each class.

Bujo ideas for decorating (optional)

The official site is clear that bullet journals don't have to be artistic or pretty; they just have to work for you. Decoration is a separate hobby that happens to share the notebook.

Bullet journal ideas by month

Monthly pages are where most people try new layouts. A few ideas that stay inside the method:

  • January: a Future Log for the year, plus a goals collection for the year
  • Any month: a one-page review on the last day, listing what you finished and what you're migrating
  • Busy months: a Weekly Log in addition to the Monthly Log
  • Quiet months: skip the weekly spread and log daily only
  • Before a holiday: a trip collection with bookings, packing and a budget

Whatever you add, write its page number in the Index the same day. A collection that isn't indexed is hard to find three months later.

Questions people ask

What should I put on the first pages of a bullet journal?

The official method starts with the Index: leave the first couple of pages blank, title them Index, and add topics and page numbers as you go. The Future Log comes next, then the Monthly Log for the current month, then daily entries.

What are good bullet journal ideas for beginners?

Set up the four core pages first, then add one collection you will use every week, such as a habit tracker, a reading list or a meal plan. Add each one to your Index so you can find it later.

What do the symbols in a bullet journal mean?

In the method's rapid logging, a dot is a task, an X is a finished task, an arrow is a task you moved, an O is an event and a dash is a note. The method also uses signifiers, such as an asterisk for priority.

How do I find a page again in a long bullet journal?

Number your pages and add each collection's topic and page numbers to the Index. If a collection continues later in the book, note the next page number beside it, which the method calls threading.

Does a bullet journal have to be decorated?

No. The method's own site says bullet journals don't have to be artistic or pretty; they have to work for you. Drawings and lettering are optional extras.
